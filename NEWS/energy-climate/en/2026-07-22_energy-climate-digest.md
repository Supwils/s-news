## I. Policy & Carbon Markets

### 1. China launches national zero-carbon factories and zero-carbon computing facilities (Policy · China)
**Summary:** Xinhua and People's Daily reported on **Jul 22, 2026** that China's Ministry of Industry and Information Technology issued a notice to organize national zero-carbon factory construction, including zero-carbon computing facilities. Eligible manufacturing firms and computing sites with a clear baseline, targets, and pathway—and a commitment to meet goals by no later than **2030**—will be selected into the national list. The program stresses technology, construction, and results, with staged acceptance (“mature one batch, certify one batch”) rather than one-off labeling. Core metrics include: carbon emissions per unit energy use no higher than **1.8** tCO2 per tce at entry and **0.2** as the target; non-fossil energy share no lower than **30%** at entry and **95%** as the target; and, for sites using over **5 GWh** of electricity a year, physically verified non-fossil power share no lower than **10%** at entry (**no** entry floor for computing facilities) and **35%** as the target.

### 2. European Commission publishes Net-Zero Industry Act non-price criteria guidance (Policy · EU)
**Summary:** On **Jul 22, 2026**, the European Commission’s Directorate-General for Energy released two guidance documents on Articles **25** (public procurement) and **26** (renewable deployment auctions) of the Net-Zero Industry Act. The guidance aims to ensure public spending rewards not only the lowest price but also environmental sustainability minima and a resilience contribution that diversifies EU cleantech supply chains. For renewable auctions, non-price criteria cover responsible business conduct, cybersecurity and data security, timely delivery, and sustainability/resilience. Obligations have applied since **Dec 30, 2025**; the guidance responds to member-state and market questions to promote consistent, proportionate implementation.

**Links:**

- [European Commission — Guidance on non-price criteria under the Net-Zero Industry Act](https://energy.ec.europa.eu/news/commission-publishes-guidance-application-non-price-criteria-under-net-zero-industry-act-2026-07-22_en)

**Commentary:** Europe’s clean-tech buying rules are shifting from “cheapest wins” to “more resilient and sustainable scores”—industrial policy written into the auction handbook.

## IV. Climate & Disasters

### 12. PNAS study: climate change made 2026 western U.S. snow drought ~4× more likely (Climate · U.S.)
**Summary:** Colorado School of Mines and The Colorado Sun reported on **Jul 22, 2026** on a PNAS study finding human-caused climate change made the western U.S. severe snow drought about **4.4 times** more likely than in a pre-industrial climate; in the Upper Colorado River Basin the factor rises to about **14 times**. Researchers estimate roughly **40 cubic kilometers** less snow-water equivalent than a similarly rare pre-industrial event—more than Lake Mead’s storage. Under a moderately high emissions path, such a drought has about a **10%** annual chance in the **2020s**, rising to about **32%** by the **2050s** and about **87%** by the **2090s**.

### 14. Japan tops 40°C, marking first official “cruelly hot day” (Disaster · East Asia)
**Summary:** BBC reported around **Jul 22, 2026** that temperatures in Tajimi, Gujo, and Toyoda surpassed **40°C**, triggering Japan’s first observed “kokushobi” (cruelly hot day)—a term the weather agency introduced in **April** for days at or above **40°C**. Nearly **300** of **914** observation points recorded “extremely hot” days (≥**35°C**), and heatstroke alerts covered more than **40** prefectures. Japan’s record high of **41.8°C** was set last year in Isesaki; authorities urged air-conditioning, hydration, salt intake, and limited outdoor activity.

**Links:**

- [BBC — Japan temperatures soar over 40C, marking first ‘cruelly hot day’](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cp3rz07grngo)

**Commentary:** When warning vocabularies have to be upgraded, it is a social signal that extreme heat is becoming the new normal—naming danger so people can hear it faster.
