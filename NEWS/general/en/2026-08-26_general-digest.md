### 1. Catastrophic Nepal–Tibet border flash flood: at least 95 dead, hundreds of foreign tourists missing (Disaster)
**Summary:** Around 9:00 a.m. local time on August 26, an ice-rock avalanche and mudslide on the Himalayan Nepal–China border sent a massive surge of mud and water from the Tibetan side into Nepal's Bhote Koshi River, destroying villages including Timure and Syabrubesi, hydropower projects, and customs facilities in Rasuwa. Nepali police said 95 bodies had been recovered and 403 people remained missing, including 341 foreign tourists (54 U.S. citizens, 33 British nationals, and others); 28 Nepali police officers were also unaccounted for. On the Tibetan side, officials reported at least three dead and 265 missing at the Gyirong port, with roads, communications, and power cut. Prime Minister Balendra Shah mobilized the army, armed police, and helicopters for rescue and warned downstream communities in Nuwakot, Dhading, and Chitwan to evacuate; experts suspect a glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) and warn a second surge is possible due to upstream blockages.

Governor Greg Abbott said the Texas Division of Emergency Management deployed additional resources and FEMA approved a Fire Management Assistance Grant. Forecasters warned highs could reach about 43°C (108°F) on Wednesday, keeping fire danger elevated.

### 5. Meta agrees to $17 billion settlement with 47 states over teen social media addiction (Tech / Regulation)
**Summary:** On August 26, attorneys general from 47 U.S. states announced that Meta agreed to pay about $17 billion and modify Facebook and Instagram to resolve federal claims that the platforms deliberately designed addictive features that worsened the youth mental health crisis. The suit alleged Meta hid product harms and illegally collected data on children under 13. The settlement requires hard daily time caps and pauses for minors, no push notifications during weekday school hours, stronger age verification and parental controls, limits on social-comparison features such as "like" counts, and tighter filtering of eating-disorder and self-harm content. California Attorney General Rob Bonta said the deal would "put an end to these dangerous practices"; Meta had not publicly commented. The case stemmed from 2021 Wall Street Journal reporting on internal documents about Instagram's harm to teenage girls.
