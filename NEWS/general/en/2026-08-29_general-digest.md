### 1. Nepal–Tibet flood toll tops 676 dead, nearly 3,000 missing; rebuild pegged at $4–5 billion (Disaster)
**Summary:** On day four after the Aug. 26 Himalayan glacier collapse triggered cross-border flash floods and mudslides, Nepal resumed search operations on Aug. 29 after a weather pause. Reuters and other outlets reported at least 669 dead and about 2,426 missing in Nepal, plus seven dead and 554 missing in Tibet’s Gyirong area—more than 676 dead and nearly 3,000 missing in total, including many foreign pilgrims and workers. Nepal’s finance minister estimated $4–5 billion for reconstruction; the foreign minister said the priority is specialist help for tunnel rescues, DNA identification, and restoring transport and communications. Chinese authorities said barrier-lake overflow risk had eased and searches continued.

### 2. Russian drone strike on Kyiv-area ammo depot sparks secondary blasts, killing at least 37 (Conflict)
**Summary:** On the evening of Aug. 28, a Russian drone hit a Ukrainian Defense Forces ammunition storage site in the village of Myla, Bucha district, Kyiv Oblast, triggering chain detonations of shells, mines, and drones. By Aug. 29, regional authorities reported at least 37 dead and 42 injured, including four children, with about 381 people evacuated; a local community head died while helping with rescues. President Zelenskyy said the depot “should not have been there” and ordered an investigation into negligence; the Defense Ministry ordered a full audit of munitions sites near homes. It is the second such secondary-explosion tragedy near Kyiv in two months.

### 3. Icelanders vote in tight referendum on restarting EU accession talks; results due Sunday (Europe)
**Summary:** Roughly 273,000 eligible Icelandic voters cast ballots on Aug. 29 on whether Iceland should resume EU accession negotiations, with polls open until 2200 GMT and results expected early Sunday. The campaign centered on fisheries sovereignty, cost of living, security, and geopolitics—including U.S. pressure over Greenland. A late Gallup poll showed a narrow lead for opponents of reopening talks. A “yes” would only authorize negotiations; actual membership would still require a second referendum, parliamentary approval, and constitutional change.

**Links:**

## II. U.S. Politics and Economy

### 4. Trump announces Venezuela oil deal claiming majority control of more than 65 billion barrels (Energy/Diplomacy)
**Summary:** Late on Aug. 28, President Trump said that, working with Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Secretary of War Pete Hegseth, Venezuelan interim President Delcy Rodriguez, and private partners, the United States had secured majority control of more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuela’s proven oil reserves “at no cost to the American taxpayer,” claiming the deal would more than double U.S. reserves and lower fuel prices. A White House official said the U.S. would receive about 55% of effective output from a new joint venture; Rodriguez said the pact covers 17 strategic fields, over $100 billion in investment, and about $209 billion in taxes. Analysts flagged legal structure, power grids, and export capacity as key uncertainties for delivery.

**Links:**

### 6. Trump’s first-ever Section 338 tariffs on Canada remain legally untested (Trade)
**Summary:** About a week ago, the administration for the first time invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 to impose up to 50% tariffs on roughly $20 billion of Canadian imports; Ottawa pledged dollar-for-dollar retaliation effective Sept. 8 after talks collapsed around Aug. 21. An Associated Press analysis published Aug. 29 notes the Depression-era authority has never been used or litigated; critics say the administration did not quantify harm and swept in goods beyond the alleged discrimination. No company has yet sued, though some legal groups are seeking plaintiffs.

**Links:**

- [ABC News — Untested in court, Trump's new tariffs on Canada raise legal questions](https://abcnews.com/Business/wireStory/untested-court-trumps-new-tariffs-canada-raise-legal-136053365)

### 8. ChangXin Memory (CXMT) sues Pentagon to exit ‘Chinese military company’ list (Tech/Sanctions)
**Summary:** China’s largest DRAM maker, ChangXin Memory Technologies, sued the U.S. Defense Department and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth in D.C. federal court on Aug. 28, seeking removal from the Pentagon’s Chinese military company list.
