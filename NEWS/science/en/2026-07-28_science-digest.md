## I. Space & Astronomy

### 1. ESO/VLT captures clearest image yet of Betelgeuse’s companion (Space)
**Summary:** A team led by Miguel Montargès of the Paris Observatory used the SPHERE instrument on ESO’s Very Large Telescope to image the red supergiant Betelgeuse in December 2024; after months of processing, they obtained the clearest direct detection yet of a candidate companion, Betelgeuse B (also called Siwarha). The results were published July 28, 2026, in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* and released by ESO. The companion appears to be about 2.6–3.1 solar masses and may help explain Betelgeuse’s roughly six-year brightness cycle; follow-up when the companion is expected on the opposite side of its orbit in late 2027 is planned to confirm orbital motion.

**Links:**

- [ESO — Astronomers find strongest evidence yet that Betelgeuse has a companion](https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2611/)
- [Scientific American — Scientists get best-yet glimpse of Betelgeuse’s companion star](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/scientists-get-best-yet-glimpse-of-betelgeuses-companion-star/)

**Commentary:** Moving Betelgeuse’s binarity from inference to imaging will reshape models of red-supergiant variability and pre-supernova evolution.

### 2. NASA completes hydrazine fueling of Roman Space Telescope ahead of Aug. 30 launch (Space)
**Summary:** On July 25, technicians at Kennedy Space Center’s Payload Hazardous Servicing Facility finished loading about 290 gallons of hydrazine into NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope; NASA’s blog posted July 27 and media followed on July 28. The propellant will power maneuvers to Sun–Earth L2 (~1 million miles) and station-keeping for a nominal five-year mission, with margin for extension and a refuelable design. NASA and SpaceX are targeting liftoff no earlier than 7:26 a.m. EDT on Aug. 30, 2026, on a Falcon Heavy from LC-39A—about nine months ahead of the original schedule.

## II. Earth Science & Climate Energy

### 4. Chicxulub dust blanket amplified a global firestorm that may have killed dinosaurs within hours (Earth Science)
**Summary:** Purdue’s Brandon Johnson and colleagues argue in the July 28 *Journal of Geophysical Research: Biogeosciences* that ultrafine impact dust acted as an insulating blanket, trapping heat from re-entering spherules and radiating it downward—boosting surface thermal flux about 3.5× versus spherules alone, enough to ignite worldwide wildfires and kill unsheltered land animals. AGU released the study the same day; related coverage cites ~1.6 trillion tonnes of dust. Authors favor acute firestorms as a major terrestrial kill mechanism, while noting incomplete global wildfire geology and ongoing debate with impact-winter starvation scenarios.

**Links:**

- [AGU Newsroom — Chicxulub impact dust set the dinosaurs’ world on fire](https://news.agu.org/press-release/chicxulub-impact-dust-set-the-dinosaurs-world-on-fire/)
- [Phys.org — Chicxulub's lingering dust may have turned falling debris into a global firestorm](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-chicxulub-lingering-falling-debris-global.html)

**Commentary:** The same dust that can “broil then chill” compresses the K-Pg kill timeline to hours and forces a rethink of which traits let survivors ride out the blast.

### 5. Austrian-led team frames an “AI–energy storage nexus” for clean transitions (Climate Energy)
**Summary:** Behnam Zakeri (Vienna University of Economics and Business) and colleagues from IIASA and KAUST outline in *Energy and Climate Change* a mutually reinforcing “AI–energy storage nexus”: ML/generative AI can speed discovery of molten salts, battery chemistries, and recycling, while AI data-center demand commercializes long-duration storage (e.g., liquid CO₂, iron–air). Storage in turn cuts peak imports, eases interconnection, and enables demand response. ESS News covered the work on July 28; authors urge grid-connection reforms, easier co-location of storage and renewables, and rewards for flexible, grid-aware computing.

**Links:**

## III. Chemistry & Basic Research

### 6. Nagoya skeletal editing yields chiral nanocarbons with CO₂-trapping pores (Chemistry)
**Summary:** Norihito Fukui and colleagues at Nagoya University report in *Nature Communications* (July 28) the first systematic application of skeletal editing—cutting and reforming interior bonds—to nanocarbon synthesis. Starting from flat π-conjugated hydrocarbons, they built gigantic chiral nanocarbons containing ten-membered rings (up to ~170 sp² carbons) and achieved regio- and enantio-selective synthesis of a helically twisted nanographene. A double-helix molecule self-assembles into a homochiral porous crystal that traps and releases CO₂; some structures retain chirality up to 280°C.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — Skeletal transformation to chiral nanocarbon molecules](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-75280-6)
- [Phys.org — Scientists cut and rebuild molecules from the inside to create new chiral nanocarbons](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-scientists-rebuild-molecules-chiral-nanocarbons.html)

**Commentary:** Importing “molecular surgery” from medicinal chemistry into nanocarbons opens chemical space peripheral expansion could not reach—gas separation and chiral optoelectronics are the near-term outlets.

### 7. 3D non-LTE model raises solar silver abundance ~55%, easing the “missing silver” puzzle (Astrophysics)
**Summary:** Uppsala University researchers, using a 3D non-LTE solar-atmosphere model, find the Sun’s silver abundance is about 55% higher than prior estimates; the work appears in *Astronomy & Astrophysics*. Xinhua / China Economic Net reported the advance on July 28: the revised value aligns better with primitive meteorites, addressing a long-standing Sun–meteorite mismatch. The team used Linköping University’s Tetralith supercomputer and plans to extend the method to other stars to trace silver’s cosmic origins.

**Links:**

### 8. Bimodal AFM watches nanoscale mechanical heterogeneities evolve in deeply aged polymer glasses (Materials)
**Summary:** A team including researchers at Kyushu University reports in *Communications Materials* (July 28) real-space observation, via bimodal atomic force microscopy, of how nanoscale mechanical heterogeneities (NMHs) evolve in an epoxy polymer glass during aging. Even ~70 K below Tg, modulus fluctuations persist in a slow, localized manner—contrasting the fast, cooperative changes near Tg—and provide spatial evidence for a recovery process faster than α-relaxation.

**Links:**

- [Communications Materials — Real-space observation of the temporal evolution of nanoscale mechanical heterogeneities in deeply aged polymer glasses](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43246-026-01305-8)

**Commentary:** Seeing why glasses keep “settling” on a nano map matters for long-life polymers and coating reliability.

## IV. Biomedicine & Health

### 9. ProMIS PMN310 Alzheimer’s Phase 1b six-month interim: no ARIA-E reported (Biomedicine)
**Summary:** On July 28, 2026, ProMIS Neurosciences released blinded six-month interim safety and biomarker data from PRECISE-AD, a Phase 1b trial of PMN310 in 136 patients with early Alzheimer’s disease: no ARIA-E (edema) cases as of the cutoff, with overall ARIA ~4.4%, all mild asymptomatic ARIA-H (microhemorrhages). PMN310 is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody designed to selectively target toxic Aβ oligomers while sparing plaques and vascular deposits. The trial remains blinded; 12-month topline results are expected in Q1 2027. STAT, an SEC 8-K, and the company release landed the same day.
