## I. Space & Deep Space

### 1. Roman Cycle 1 selects 118 programs as Aug. 30 launch nears (astronomy)
**Summary:** Scientific American reported on August 27 that NASA selected 118 Cycle 1 General Investigator programs from 374 proposals as the community science core for the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope’s first year—61 small, 45 medium, and 12 large programs, plus five General Astrophysics Surveys totaling about 1,456 hours. Roman’s field of view is roughly 100 times that of Hubble and is expected to produce about 1.4 TB of science data per day and roughly 500 TB per year. NASA and SpaceX still target liftoff no earlier than 7:26 a.m. EDT on August 30 from Kennedy Space Center LC-39A on a Falcon Heavy, with a Launch Readiness Review set for August 28.

## II. Synthetic Biology & Biomedicine

### 4. Harvard’s AGENTEX: cell-free platform expands custom proteins to 34 amino acids (synthetic biology)
**Summary:** George Church’s lab at Harvard Medical School and the Wyss Institute reported AGENTEX (automated genetic tRNA expansion) in Nature on August 26: engineered tRNAs and ribosomes in cell-free lysates let researchers build proteins with up to 34 amino acids instead of nature’s 20, without genome recoding or living organisms. A key finding overturns dogma that tRNA tails must end in CCA—tSCAN identified usable nonstandard ends such as CGA that pair with orthogonal engineered ribosomes. The workflow runs on Opentrons robots with open software; applications include novel protein drugs and materials, though in-cell adaptation remains open.

**Links:**

- [Wyss Institute — Researchers clear major obstacle in genetic engineering of proteins](https://wyss.harvard.edu/news/researchers-clear-major-obstacle-in-genetic-engineering-of-proteins/)
- [Nature — Automated prototyping of genetic codes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10949-y)

**Commentary:** Moving genetic-code expansion from “edit the cell” to “assemble in a tube” could rewrite both the safety boundary and the iteration speed of synthetic biology.

### 5. Pan-rhinovirus vaccine APL-10456 Phase 1 Part A: single dose drives cross-species immunity (vaccines)
**Summary:** Imperial College London and Apollo Therapeutics’ candidate APL-10456 completed enrollment and dosing in a 144-volunteer randomized placebo-controlled Phase 1 trial; Part A (ages 18–54, three single-dose levels) showed good tolerability and broad cross-reactive cellular immunity against all three major rhinovirus species (A/B/C). Part B (≥55, two-dose regimen) data are expected in Q4 2026, with a COPD-patient study planned for the first half of 2027. Rhinoviruses comprise roughly 180 strains and still lack an approved vaccine or specific antiviral; the adjuvanted recombinant protein subunit shot targets asthma and COPD exacerbations.

**Links:**

- [Imperial College London — Early trials for ‘common cold’ vaccine offer encouraging signs](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/news/articles/medicine/nhli/2026/early-trials-for-common-cold-vaccine-offer-encouraging-signs/)
- [Xinhua — Rhinovirus vaccine Phase 1 shows encouraging early results](https://www.news.cn/world/20260827/aef6147ecb5547f290e4b31ecefd75eb/c.html)

**Commentary:** A “common cold” vaccine fails on strain diversity first—Phase 1 shows cross-species immunity is inducible; the harder question is fewer COPD flare-ups in patients.

### 6. CAS team’s self-contracting myografts mimic exercise benefits in mice (regenerative medicine)
**Summary:** Researchers including teams at the Chinese Academy of Sciences’ Institute of Stem Cell and Regenerative Medicine reported in Nature Aging on August 26 that subcutaneously transplanted autologous differentiated myocytes form vascularized, spontaneously contracting myografts. Older mice showed higher lean-mass fraction by about eight weeks, higher bone density by about 15 weeks, better running distance and grip strength, plus systemic inflammation and metabolic shifts; obese models also saw blunted rises in body fat and blood glucose. Grafts can also act as living biofactories for proteins such as parathyroid hormone and growth hormone. Authors stress human safety and pharmacology are unproven—this remains a proof of concept.

**Links:**

- [Nature Aging — Contractile myografts confer systemic anti-aging benefits](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-026-01190-3)
- [Nature — This pulsating muscle graft mimics benefits of exercise](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-02664-5)

**Commentary:** If an “exercise-factor factory” can live under the skin, care for bed rest and sarcopenia may expand beyond urging activity—to an implantable metabolic add-on, if human trials clear safety first.

## III. Climate, Environment & Energy

### 7. Lake sediments suggest historical black carbon was underestimated, potentially amplifying Arctic snowmelt (climate)
**Summary:** On August 27 the Institute of Earth Environment, Chinese Academy of Sciences, described work combining Chinese lake-sediment soot fluxes with CESM modeling: early-to-mid 20th-century sediment records stayed relatively stable while inventory-driven simulations ran low, implying historical black-carbon inventories may be underestimated. Under a sediment-constrained enhanced-emission scenario, spring–summer radiative forcing north of 60°N strengthened, with Arctic mean surface warming of about 0.13 K, top-of-atmosphere net solar radiation up about 1.05 W·m⁻², and local summer warming up to about 1.5 K in places. The study appears in Communications Earth & Environment.

**Links:**

- [IEECAS — Historical black-carbon underestimation and potential Arctic snowmelt impacts](https://ieexa.cas.cn/news/kydt/202608/t20260827_8265744.html)
- [Communications Earth & Environment — Sediment records reveal elevated black carbon emissions potentially amplifying Arctic snowmelt](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43247-026-03654-1)

**Commentary:** Climate attribution is not only greenhouse gases—if the black-carbon ledger is systematically low, Arctic albedo feedback needs another historical page rewritten.

### 8. NSYSU: mangrove and seagrass blue-carbon signals detected at 1 km depth, fluxes 2.7–4.2× model (ocean carbon)
**Summary:** National Sun Yat-sen University’s Hung Chia-chang team reported on August 27 that 2023 northwest Pacific warm-pool sampling at five stations recovered eDNA and isotope signals of macroalgae, mangroves, and seagrass at 500 m and 1,000 m—some sites roughly 900–1,200 km from land. Particulate organic-carbon flux at 1,000 m ran about 2.7–4.2 times higher than classic Martin Curve predictions, indicating coastal blue carbon can travel laterally on currents before sinking. Results are in Global Biogeochemical Cycles; exact source regions remain unresolved.

**Links:**

- [UDN — Blue carbon not only coastal: NSYSU finds kilometer-depth signals](https://udn.com/news/story/6928/9717393)
- [China Times — Mangrove signals spotted in the deep sea](https://www.chinatimes.com/realtimenews/20260827002609-260412)

**Commentary:** Blue-carbon accounts that only tally wetland stocks miss the “ride the current, then sink” pathway—carbon maps need a horizontal-transport dimension.

### 9. Flinders zinc–iodine aqueous battery tops 60,000 lab cycles; prototyping underway (energy storage)
**Summary:** Flinders University’s Zhongfan Jia team reported in Angewandte Chemie an aqueous zinc–iodine battery that cages polyhalide anions in cross-linked polycyclodextrin to curb polyiodide shuttling. Fully charged in seven minutes it can run at about 1.3–1.4 V and 200 mAh/g for ~8,000 cycles; charged in three minutes at 150 mAh/g it exceeds 60,000 cycles, with fade of roughly 0.0001%–0.0003% per cycle. The group is working with industry on a prototyping platform aimed at large-scale storage alternatives to lithium-ion. ESS News and others followed on August 27.

**Links:**

**Commentary:** Grid storage wants cheap, safe, and durable—60,000 cycles is a lab ceiling signal; manufacturing and system-level fade are the next gates.

## IV. Fundamental Research

### 11. Long-read sequencing: some sperm gene conversion occurs before meiosis (genetics)
**Summary:** Teams including the Wellcome Sanger Institute, University of Cambridge, and Tel Aviv University reported in Nature on August 26 that high-fidelity long reads from 15 sperm samples of 13 donors yielded 2,382 candidate non-crossover gene-conversion events; a subset differs from PRDM9-driven meiotic recombination and associates with genomic fragile sites. Similar non-crossover patterns appear in blood with almost no crossovers. The work suggests a substantial share of sperm diversity arises from pre-meiotic DNA repair, with donor-to-donor variation even among shared PRDM9 genotypes. ScienceTimes covered the story on August 27.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Long-read sequencing reveals pre-meiotic gene conversion in sperm](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10901-0)
- [ScienceTimes — Human Sperm Gain Genetic Diversity Before Meiosis Begins](https://www.sciencetimes.com/articles/62495/20260827/human-sperm-gain-genetic-diversity-before-meiosis-begins-new-study-finds.htm)

**Commentary:** Textbooks book almost all recombination to meiosis—adding lifelong pre-meiotic repair in the spermatogonial line redraws one corner of the inherited-variation map.

### 12. Sliding-electrified water drops can breach coatings and drive metal corrosion (physical chemistry)
**Summary:** A Nature paper published August 26 shows that water drops sliding on insulating surfaces can charge spontaneously—potentials can exceed 1 kV—and that sequences of charged drops hitting coated metal can electrically break down the coating and trigger corrosion. The authors argue this overlooked mechanism may matter for buildings, ships, cars, and cultural heritage, and that anticorrosion strategies should consider materials that resist charge-induced damage.

**Links:**

- [Nature — Spontaneously charged water drops induce corrosion](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10941-6)

**Commentary:** Corrosion chapters that only teach electrochemical cells miss a physical branch: raindrops that charge themselves and punch through paint.
