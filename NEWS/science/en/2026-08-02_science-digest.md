## I. Space & Planetary Science

### 1. Curiosity finds a vast field of honeycomb polygons in Valle Grande (Space)
**Summary:** While climbing Valle Grande in Gale Crater, NASA’s Curiosity rover captured a 340-frame Mastcam panorama on sols 4930–4931 (June 19–20, 2026) showing the densest polygonal fracture field of the mission: features about 4–8 cm across tiling the terrain and wrapping a ~6 m sand-capped butte nicknamed Miraflores. Polygons can form from drying mud, temperature cycling, burial compaction, or mineral dehydration; the team is combining shape and chemistry measurements to pin down the process and reconstruct the ancient water environment. Curiosity landed in 2012 and has already shown that early Mars had water, chemistry, and nutrients that could have supported microbes.

**Commentary:** A sea of regular cracks is not scenery—it is a process log etched in rock; once the formation path is fixed, Valle Grande’s hydrology story gets rewritten.

### 2. Chang’e-6 farside impact melts favor a long decline over a late heavy spike (Planetary)
**Summary:** A Curtin University–led team with Chinese Academy of Sciences collaborators published ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar ages of Chang’e-6 impact-melt rocks in *Science Advances* on July 23, with SciTechDaily and others amplifying the result on August 2. The fragments record impacts spanning about 4.33 to 1.13 billion years ago—more than three billion years of lunar bombardment—and support a gradual decline in early impact flux rather than a short Late Heavy Bombardment spike near 3.9 Ga. The lunar farside, less reworked by later geology than the nearside, offers a cleaner archive against which nearside-dominated chronologies can be tested.

**Links:**

- [Curtin University — Rocks from unexplored far side of the moon help rewrite lunar history](https://www.curtin.edu.au/news/media-release/rocks-from-unexplored-far-side-of-the-moon-help-rewrite-lunar-history/)
- [Science Advances — Bombardment history on the lunar farside (doi:10.1126/sciadv.aee8718)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aee8718)

**Commentary:** Farside samples free the cataclysm-versus-decay debate from nearside sampling bias—early Solar System dynamics are being rewritten by returned rock.

### 3. Spent Falcon 9 upper stage enters final countdown to lunar impact (Space)
**Summary:** ABC News and other outlets reported around August 2 that the Falcon 9 upper stage (~4 tonnes) that launched Firefly’s Blue Ghost-1 and ispace’s Resilience in January 2025 is still on track to strike the Moon’s near side near Einstein crater around 06:35 UTC on August 5, 2026 (~2:34 a.m. ET) at roughly 5,400 mph (~2.43 km/s). Orbital solutions from Bill Gray’s Project Pluto remain the main public reference; any impact flash will be extremely faint, but LRO, Danuri, and some amateur telescopes will try to catch the plume and crater. The event poses no Earth hazard and offers a rare open-air experiment in artificial lunar impact mechanics.

## II. Astrophysics & Fundamental Research

### 4. LHAASO confirms Cygnus X-3 as a ≥30 PeV super-PeVatron (Astrophysics)
**Summary:** China’s Institute of High Energy Physics (CAS) announced via Xinhua on August 2 that LHAASO has confirmed the compact-object binary Cygnus X-3 can accelerate cosmic-ray particles to at least 30 PeV—well above the ~1 PeV Galactic ceiling long assumed by mainstream theory. Ultra-high-energy gamma rays show month-scale variability and a ~4.8-hour orbital periodicity, localizing the accelerator to a region roughly three solar radii across. Results appear in *National Science Review* (*Cygnus X-3: A variable petaelectronvolt γ-ray source*). It is the first UHE gamma-ray source with clear temporal variability, opening a new window on extreme physics near black holes or neutron stars and multi-messenger follow-up.

**Links:**

**Commentary:** The Galactic accelerator ceiling just moved—variability pinning PeV physics to a resolvable jet core changes the map.

### 5. Earth’s inner core may host a radial gradient of superionic hydrogen (Geophysics)
**Summary:** Free-energy calculations by teams at Xiamen University and Columbia University, published in *PNAS* and covered by ScienceAlert on August 2, show hydrogen can exist in a superionic state in the iron lattice under inner-core conditions—flowing like a liquid while conducting electricity. Phase diagrams at different inner-core pressures collapse when temperature is scaled to pure-iron melting, implying solid–liquid partitioning is controlled mainly by reduced temperature and is weakly pressure-sensitive. The work yields a radial hydrogen gradient that drops sharply from the inner-core boundary toward the center and can drive continuous redistribution into the outer core; the inner core still grows at roughly 1 mm per year.

**Links:**

- [ScienceAlert — Earth's Inner Core Could Be Full Of Extreme Hydrogen](https://www.sciencealert.com/earths-inner-core-could-be-full-of-extreme-hydrogen-unlike-anything-on-the-surface)
- [PubMed — Radial gradient of superionic hydrogen in the Earth's inner core](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42479837/)

**Commentary:** Light elements are no longer a uniform average mix—radial gradients give chemical buoyancy, the geodynamo, and inner-core growth a shared thermodynamic handle.

### 6. FDA-cleared Freenome SimpleScreen adds a blood option for CRC screening (Medicine)
**Summary:** Medical News Today on August 2 reviewed FDA approval (July 2026) of Freenome’s SimpleScreen CRC blood-based screen for average-risk adults 45 and older. In the PREEMPT CRC study of more than 48,000 people, the test detected roughly 8 in 10 colorectal cancers and returned negative results for about 9 in 10 people without CRC or advanced precancerous lesions. Abbott will exclusively commercialize the test in the U.S. this fall, triggering a ~$100 million milestone payment; the approval meets Medicare coverage criteria and joins Guardant’s Shield and stool/colonoscopy options for people who decline preferred guideline tests.

### 10. PNAS: Lake-ice loss sensitivity can jump 22× past winter temperature thresholds (Climate)
**Summary:** An international team led by NIGLAS (CAS) analyzed ice phenology and air temperatures for 724 Northern Hemisphere lakes (2000–2022), publishing in *PNAS* on July 27 with August 2 follow-up coverage. Once mean winter air temperature exceeds thresholds of about −13.7°C to −6.8°C, phenological sensitivity of ice loss can rise up to 22-fold; spring ice-off responds far more strongly to warming than autumn freeze-up. Under a high-emissions scenario, winter ice seasons could shorten by ~40 days by century’s end, and the share of lakes past critical thresholds could rise from ~23% to ~70%. Thresholds are governed mainly by large-scale climate and albedo, not individual lake morphometry.

**Links:**

- [Phys.org — Lake ice loss could accelerate after winter temperatures cross critical thresholds](https://phys.org/news/2026-07-lake-ice-loss-winter-temperatures.html)
- [NIGLAS — Critical Temperature Thresholds for Lake Ice Cover Loss](http://english.niglas.cas.cn/ns_25084/es_20226/202607/t20260728_1179239.html)

**Commentary:** Ice loss is not a linear slide—past the threshold, freshwater ecology and winter water planning must be recalculated for a different regime.

### 11. Brazilian study: Human warming may compress 3.3 Myr of tropical vegetation change into decades (Climate)
**Summary:** UFMG’s Remote Sensing Center reconstructed tropical vegetation over 3.3 million years and projected change to 2050 in the *Journal of Biogeography*; The Conversation and The Cool Down highlighted the work on August 2. When the planet was ~4°C warmer ~3.2 Ma, Amazonia was less than half its present extent and the Atlantic Forest shrank below 5% of potential range; ~22% of the tropics kept stable climate–vegetation refugia. Under the highest-emissions path, tropics may warm 2–4°C by 2050, with Amazonia possibly losing more than a third of its forest and the Malay Archipelago more than 40%—magnitudes comparable to millions of years of natural change, but compressed into decades.

**Links:**

### 12. Record-low Danube forces Hungary’s Paks nuclear plant into first full shutdown (Energy/Climate)
**Summary:** Prime Minister Peter Magyar said on August 2 that Hungary’s only nuclear plant, Paks—supplying nearly half of national electricity—would power down for the first time in 44 years because record-low Danube levels cut cooling intake. Output had fallen to about 965 MW on Friday and 240 MW overnight versus a normal ~2,000 MW; a river level of −134 cm at Paks forces full shutdown, with forecasts as low as −144 cm versus the 2018 record of −98 cm. Heat and drought have dropped European river levels and put more than 100 Hungarian towns under water-use limits; Slovakia offered power support.

**Links:**

**Commentary:** Low-carbon baseload meets a climate–hydrology floor—cooling water is not an infinite commons, and drought years rewrite the power-security ledger.
