## I. Space & Deep Space

### 1. Long March-10B Maiden Flight: China's First Controlled Recovery of an Orbital Rocket Booster

**Summary:** According to Xinhua and CGTN on July 10, 2026, the Long March-10B (CZ-10B) lifted off at 12:15 from the Hainan Commercial Space Launch Site on its debut flight and placed its payload into the planned orbit. About six minutes after first- and second-stage separation, the first stage returned vertically and was captured by a net system on the seaborne platform Linghangzhe (Pathfinder), marking China's first controlled recovery of an orbital-class booster. The two-stage, 5-meter-diameter reusable vehicle uses seven LOX-kerosene engines on the first stage and offers roughly 16 tonnes of reusable payload capacity to low Earth orbit. Its hook-and-net sea capture differs from SpaceX's grid-fin leg landing and is described as the world's first successful net-based orbital booster recovery. The team aims to re-fly the recovered stage by year-end and validate technologies for the broader Long March-10 crewed lunar family.

**Commentary:** Reusable launch is moving from "land it once" to "fly it again" — if re-flight succeeds, net capture could cut constellation costs while feeding data into China's lunar rocket program.

### 2. NASA IXPE Maps Magnetic Fields of the 'Lighthouse' Pulsar Nebula for the First Time

**Summary:** Per NASA (July 9, 2026) and a paper in The Astrophysical Journal (DOI: 10.3847/1538-4357/ae64f3), a team led by Stanford undergraduate Jack Dinsmore used NASA's Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE) to observe PSR J1101−6101 in the Lighthouse Nebula for roughly one megasecond. With >99% confidence, they confirmed that magnetic fields in the nebula filament align with the flow of high-energy particles; polarization degree was ~55%±18%, implying weaker local turbulence than some models predict. X-ray and radio polarizations are nearly orthogonal, suggesting electrons of different energies occupy distinct regions and multiple acceleration mechanisms may coexist.

**Links:**

- [NASA — NASA Space Telescope Maps Magnetic Fields of 'Lighthouse' Pulsar](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/ixpe/nasa-space-telescope-maps-magnetic-fields-of-lighthouse-pulsar/)
- [The Astrophysical Journal — IXPE Polarizations of the Lighthouse Pulsar, Trail, and Filament](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4357/ae64f3)

**Commentary:** Polarimetry turns pulsar trails from "visible" to "measurable" — multi-wavelength magnetic mismatches offer testable constraints on particle acceleration and galactic field transport.

## II. Biomedicine & Health

### 3. FDA Broadens Padcev plus Keytruda Approval to All MIBC Patients in Perioperative Setting

**Summary:** Per Pfizer and Astellas announcements on July 10, 2026, and CancerNetwork reporting, the U.S. FDA approved enfortumab vedotin (Padcev) plus pembrolizumab (Keytruda, including subcutaneous Qlex) for neoadjuvant and adjuvant treatment of adult muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) **regardless of cisplatin eligibility**, making it the first platinum-free perioperative regimen for the full MIBC population. Approval rests on the Phase 3 KEYNOTE-B15/EV-304 trial (NCT04700124): among 808 treatment-naive patients, the combination cut event-free survival risk by ~47% (HR 0.53) and death risk by ~35% (HR 0.65) versus neoadjuvant gemcitabine plus cisplatin. A cisplatin-ineligible indication was approved in November 2025 (EV-303).

### 6. Tree Frog Gut Bacterium Ewingella americana: 100% Complete Response in Mouse Colorectal Cancer

**Summary:** Per ScienceDaily on July 10, 2026, citing work from the Japan Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (JAIST) and a Gut Microbes paper (DOI: 10.1080/19490976.2025.2599562), Eijiro Miyako's team isolated 45 bacterial strains from Japanese tree frogs and other amphibians/reptiles; nine showed antitumor activity, with Ewingella americana strongest. A single intravenous dose (~10⁹ CFU) in a Colon-26 syngeneic mouse model produced 100% complete tumor regression, outperforming anti-PD-L1 and liposomal doxorubicin controls. The bacterium preferentially proliferates in hypoxic tumors, combining direct cytotoxicity with immune activation; it was undetectable in healthy organs after 24 hours with no chronic toxicity over 60 days.

## III. Climate & Environment

### 7. South Australian HAB Culprit Karenia cristata Among Most Toxic Harmful Microalgae Studied

**Summary:** Per a Nature Ecology & Evolution paper published July 6, 2026 (DOI: 10.1038/s41559-026-03115-0) and phys.org coverage, Shauna Murray's team at the University of Technology Sydney confirmed that a harmful algal bloom starting March 2025 and persisting >15 months across ~20,000 km² of South Australia was dominated by Karenia cristata, producing brevetoxins (BTX-2, BTX-3, BTX-B5) with a profile distinct from Florida's Karenia brevis. Lab assays show toxicity an order of magnitude above previously studied species — as few as five cells per milliliter kill half of tested invertebrates — consistent with field concentrations often exceeding one million cells per liter, >1 million marine animal deaths across >600 taxa, and a bloom still not fully dissipated in 2026.

**Links:**

### 8. Microplastics Settle Faster When Nearby: Drafting Effect Alters Hotspot Predictions

**Summary:** Per an AIP SciLight on July 10, 2026, and a Physics of Fluids paper (DOI: 10.1063/5.0334330), Siamak Seyfi and colleagues at TU Delft tracked spherical microplastic settling in quiescent tanks and found that when two particles are close, the trailing particle generally accelerates toward the leader — subtle hydrodynamic interactions alter settling dynamics. Near-source hotspots such as wastewater outfalls, models ignoring inter-particle effects may systematically underestimate settling flux and sediment accumulation.

**Links:**

- [AIP — Microplastics settle more quickly when near one another](https://www.aip.org/scilights/microplastics-settle-more-quickly-when-near-one-another)
- [Physics of Fluids — Settling and drafting of spheres in quiescent fluids in microplastic relevant regimes](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0334330)

**Commentary:** Microplastic fate cannot rely on single-particle Stokes settling alone — collective hydrodynamics near discharge points create underestimated deposition hotspots.

## IV. Fundamental Research

### 9. Ancient Human DNA Recovered from Cave Walls for the First Time

**Summary:** Per ScienceAlert on July 10, 2026, and a Nature Communications paper (DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-74234-2), the First Art project team sampled 120 specimens from 24 rock-art panels in 11 caves across Spain and Portugal. For the first time, ancient human mitochondrial and nuclear DNA was recovered from cave walls — pigmented and unpigmented surfaces alike — with only five positives. Samples from Escoural and Covarón caves lacking faunal DNA suggest direct deposition via saliva or sweat. Covarón DNA maps to a Western hunter-gatherer cluster common in Iberia; while not proving artist identity, calcite crusts and protected conditions can preserve human genetic traces for millennia on rock surfaces.

**Links:**

- [ScienceAlert — For The First Time, Ancient Human DNA Has Been Found Preserved on Cave Walls](https://www.sciencealert.com/for-the-first-time-ancient-human-dna-has-been-found-preserved-on-cave-walls)
- [Nature Communications — Investigating ancient human DNA preservation on cave walls and in rock art](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-74234-2)

**Commentary:** Prehistoric demography need not depend only on bones — cave walls become sampleable genetic archives, opening non-invasive population reconstruction at sites without burials.

### 10. Wet, Low-Oxygen Burial Drives Oxidative Cross-Linking: Experimental Validation of Ancient Brain Preservation

**Summary:** Per Greek Reporter on July 10, 2026, Alexandra Morton-Hayward's team at the University of Oxford buried mice under four moisture/oxygen conditions for six months. Brains in wet, hypoxic settings retained far more protein than oxygen-rich controls, with key chemical changes beginning within weeks of death. The proposed oxidative cross-linking mechanism — limited oxygen causes oxidation to form stable covalent bonds between proteins rather than destroy them — aligns with prior hypotheses from >4,400 archaeological brain specimens involving metal-catalyzed molecular cross-linking, potentially explaining cases like the Heslington brain surviving as the sole soft tissue in otherwise skeletonized burials.

**Links:**

- [Greek Reporter — Scientists Solve the Mystery of Why Ancient Human Brains Survive for Thousands of Years](https://greekreporter.com/2026/07/10/ancient-brain-survive-thousands-years/)
- [Scientific American — Why These Millennia-Old Brains Are So Well Preserved](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-these-millennia-old-brains-are-so-well-preserved/)

**Commentary:** Decay and preservation are not opposites — under the right oxidative chemistry in lipid- and iron-rich brain tissue, decomposition pathways lock into durable polymers, with unexpected parallels for forensic and neurodegeneration research.

### 11. Record 'Invisible Gold' in Seafloor Pyrite at Japan's Higashi-Aogashima Caldera

**Summary:** Per ScienceAlert on July 10, 2026, and a Scientific Reports paper (DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-58760-z), researchers from Shizuoka University, Waseda University, and the University of Tokyo used secondary-ion mass spectrometry (SIMS, ~7 ppb detection limit) on hydrothermal sulfides from the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera in the Izu-Ogasawara arc. Colloform pyrite in black-smoker chimneys contains up to ~1.9 wt% "invisible gold" (atomic incorporation or nanoparticles within the crystal lattice) — the highest reported concentration in seafloor sulfides globally. The site lies ~350 km south of Tokyo, discovered in 2015, and sits amid growing debate over protecting active vents versus commercial seabed mining.

### 12. Soft Lithium Dendrites Fracture Hard Garnet Electrolytes via a 'Waterjet' Mechanism

**Summary:** Per ScienceDaily on July 10, 2026, citing Max Planck Institute for Sustainable Materials work and a Nature paper (DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10415-9), Yuwei Zhang's team used multiscale cryogenic electron microscopy and fracture models to show soft lithium dendrites generate hydrostatic pressure inside existing microcracks that brittle-fractures garnet solid electrolytes, rather than pre-enriching lithium nuclei at crack tips. Geometric voids to redirect dendrite growth, grain-boundary toughening, and defect engineering offer actionable anti-short-circuit strategies for all-solid-state lithium-metal batteries.

### 13. First Bose-Einstein Condensate of Sodium-Rubidium Polar Molecules

**Summary:** Per a Nature Physics paper published July 9, 2026 (DOI: 10.1038/s41567-026-03362-9), Shi Zhan and colleagues at the Chinese University of Hong Kong used dual microwave shielding to suppress two-body collisional loss, cooling ground-state NaRb molecules in an optical trap to quantum degeneracy with ~500 molecules in a BEC. Tuning dipolar interactions, they observed both gas-phase condensates and self-bound quantum droplets. Polar molecules offer electric dipole moments and rich rotational structure for studying strongly dipolar quantum matter.

### 14. Spontaneous Current Loops in Kagome Metal CsV3Sb5 Point to Hidden Quantum Order

**Summary:** Per a Nature Physics paper published June 17, 2026 (DOI: 10.1038/s41567-026-03339-8) and phys.org coverage, researchers including Kyoto University used high-precision local spectroscopy to detect tiny intrinsic magnetic fields from spontaneous loop currents in the kagome metal CsV3Sb5, providing microscopic evidence for an imaginary charge density wave (imaginary CDW) and time-reversal symmetry breaking. The electronic state connects to the pseudogap phase of high-temperature cuprate superconductors, offering a new lens on hidden order in strongly correlated systems.

**Links:**

- [Nature Physics — Microscopic signatures of an imaginary charge density wave in a kagome metal](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41567-026-03339-8)
