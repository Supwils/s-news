### 2. Juno records plasma-wave fine structure at Jupiter’s bow shock (Space)
**Summary:** Joseph, Kurth, Wilson and colleagues reported on July 31 in *Nature Communications* plasma-wave observations from NASA’s Juno spacecraft at the Jovian bow shock, characterizing wave activity and energy dissipation near the shock interface. The bow shock is a key boundary between the solar wind and a planetary magnetosphere; in situ high-frequency diagnostics at Jupiter’s scale have been comparatively limited. The new measurements supply directly constrained wave evidence for giant-planet magnetosphere–solar-wind coupling and enable cross-scale comparison with terrestrial and other planetary bow shocks.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — Plasma wave observations from Juno spacecraft at the Jovian bow shock](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-76223-x)

**Commentary:** Moving giant-planet shocks from remote inference to in situ spectral diagnostics gives space-weather models for outer planets a harder observational anchor.

## II. Fundamental Research & Quantum Materials

### 3. Extensible photonic quantum computing integrates nonlinearity and generates optical GKP states quasi-deterministically (Quantum)
**Summary:** Yu, Sun, Chen et al. reported on July 31 in *Nature Photonics* an extensible photonic platform that seamlessly combines fully programmable linear-optical networks with integrated nonlinear Kerr modules, supporting a universal physical gate set. The team demonstrated quasi-deterministic generation of optical Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill (GKP) states—key resources for bosonic error correction previously realized mostly probabilistically—and simulated many-body dynamics such as the Bose–Hubbard model, tasks long considered beyond linear-optics-only photonic hardware. The authors argue the architecture offers a viable route toward photonic quantum simulation and fault-tolerant computing.

**Links:**

- [Nature Photonics — Extensible universal photonic quantum computing with nonlinearity](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41566-026-01962-8)

**Commentary:** Photonics’ missing piece has been scalable strong nonlinearity—welding nonlinear modules into programmable linear networks is closer to universality than stacking more interferometers alone.

### 4. Direct real-space observation of braiding Majorana-like zero modes (Physics)
**Summary:** Ma, He, Deng et al. reported on July 31 in *Nature Communications* the identification of boundary-bound Majorana-like zero modes (BMLZMs) in an artificial lattice, adiabatically transported along the sample boundary by controlled Kekulé-phase modulation. By mapping acoustic-field evolution they reconstructed the full real-space trajectory and observed phase accumulation characteristic of braiding. Non-Abelian braiding of Majorana zero modes is central to topological quantum computation, yet direct real-space imaging of the full braiding process has remained elusive; this acoustic/artificial-lattice analog closes part of the gap between theory and observable dynamics.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — Direct observation of braiding of Majorana-like zero modes in real space](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-75765-4)

**Commentary:** Even on an analog platform, filming the braiding trajectory is a stronger physical narrative for topological logic than detecting a zero-bias peak alone.

### 5. High-field-stabilized reentrant superconductivity in infinite-layer nickelate films (Condensed Matter)
**Summary:** Rubi, Yip, Krenkel et al. demonstrated on July 31 in *Nature Communications* high-field-stabilized reentrant superconductivity in infinite-layer nickelate thin films that can reach transition temperatures up to about 40 K. They interpret both low-field and high-field superconducting states through a Jaccarino–Peter field-compensation mechanism, showing that substantially enhanced effective upper critical fields may be achievable in higher-Tc materials. Prior examples of field-induced superconductivity were largely confined to low-Tc Chevrel phases, organic conductors, uranium heavy-fermion systems, or moiré graphene.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — High-field-stabilized reentrant superconductivity in infinite-layer nickelate thin films](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-75922-9)

**Commentary:** The nickelate story is shifting from “does it superconduct?” to “how does it survive strong fields?”—a hard test for both applications and pairing mechanisms.

### 6. *Nature Reviews Chemistry*: the past, present and future of self-driving laboratories (Chemistry)
**Summary:** Canty and Abolhasani published a July 31 review in *Nature Reviews Chemistry* tracing a decade of self-driving laboratories (SDLs)—from narrowly focused automation to multipurpose discovery platforms in which algorithms propose, execute and interpret experiments with limited human intervention. They frame the next phase around three interdependent requirements: scalability, generalizability and provenance-complete experimentation, stressing interoperable data and metadata standards, modular hardware, and trustworthy AI agents that reason under uncertainty within rigorous safety and ethical bounds if SDLs are to become shared scientific infrastructure.

**Links:**

- [Nature Reviews Chemistry — The past, present and future of self-driving laboratories](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41570-026-00847-2)

**Commentary:** The bottleneck is no longer whether a robot arm can pipette—it is provenance and cross-lab transferability, or SDLs remain faster silos.

### 8. Novo Nordisk ZEUS Phase 3: IL-6 inhibitor ziltivekimab fails to cut major adverse cardiovascular events (Medicine)
**Summary:** Novo Nordisk announced on July 31 headline results from the ZEUS cardiovascular outcomes Phase 3 trial: in more than 6,300 people with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD) and inflammation (hsCRP ≥2 mg/L), once-monthly ziltivekimab 15 mg did not reduce MACE (CV death, non-fatal MI or non-fatal stroke) versus placebo (hazard ratio 0.99; 95% CI 0.88–1.11). The drug lowered free IL-6 and hsCRP as expected, confirming target engagement, but biomarker improvements did not translate into event benefit. The company said the outcome does not change its 2026 adjusted operating-profit outlook but will trigger a non-cash impairment in Q3 2026; heart-failure HERMES and acute-MI ARTEMIS trials continue.

### 10. Mummy viral genomes confirm European origin of smallpox in the Americas (Biology)
**Summary:** Researchers from Trinity College Dublin, the University of Chile and collaborators reported in *Science* (published July 30; widely covered July 31) the first ancient variola (smallpox) genomes recovered in the Americas, from two individuals at Chile’s Camarones site dated roughly 1492–1631. Phylogeny places the lineage between medieval and later European strains, splitting from a European branch around the thirteenth century and arguing against a pre-colonial American origin—molecular confirmation of introduction during European colonization. The work also discusses gene loss during adaptation to human hosts, with implications for related orthopoxviruses such as mpox.

### 11. UK postwar sugar rationing natural experiment links early-life limits to lower disease and mortality risk (Health)
**Summary:** Zheng, Zhou, Huang et al. reported on July 31 in *Nature Communications* an analysis of 64,809 UK Biobank participants treating Britain’s postwar sugar rationing as a natural experiment. Exposure during roughly the first 1,000 days of life was associated with about 9% lower incidence of hallmark ageing-related disease (HR 0.91; 95% CI 0.88–0.94) and about 19% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.81; 95% CI 0.69–0.93); mediation analysis attributed ~60% of the survival association to differences in incident disease. Rationed individuals appeared 1.0–1.2 years younger across multiple biological-age clocks, with lower organ ages (notably lung, heart and liver) and proteomic enrichment of AMPK/longevity pathways plus mTOR suppression. The authors note consistency with WHO and related free-sugar guidance.

**Links:**

- [Nature Communications — Early life sugar rationing and ageing related diseases, biological ageing and mortality](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-76257-1)

**Commentary:** Nutrition policy history becomes lifelong health evidence—infant-food and sugar-tax debates gain a long-horizon natural-experiment weight.

## IV. Climate & Environmental Health

### 13. Circumglobal wave “relay” helps explain why Northwestern Pacific marine heatwaves refuse to fade (Climate)
**Summary:** Research by Jin-Yi Yu (National Central University) and Yu Zhao (UC Irvine) in *Science Advances*, widely covered in Chinese-language media on July 31, argues Northwestern Pacific marine heatwaves are not driven only by local high pressure or reduced cloud cover. Instead they couple to a quasistationary wavenumber-5 circumglobal wave (CGW) spanning Eurasia–North Pacific–North Atlantic and can operate largely independently of ENSO. Summer CGW modulation of surface heat fluxes feeds a self-reinforcing loop in which warming intensifies the CGW and prolongs heatwaves; summer northern North Atlantic warming can persist into winter via oceanic thermal inertia and excite a Eurasian wave train that sustains events across seasons and into multi-year episodes. The team draws on 150+ years of observations and dozens of climate models, noting stronger summer CGW activity since the 1970s.

**Links:**

- [Ubrand / United Daily News — Why marine heatwaves persist](https://ubrand.udn.com/ubrand/story/123658/9646554)
- [UCI PDF — Trans-basin linkages prolong Northwestern Pacific marine heatwaves](https://www.ess.uci.edu/~yu/PDF/Zhao_%20and_Yu.Science_Advances.2026.pdf)

**Commentary:** Recasting regional SST extremes as a cross-basin atmospheric relay could rewrite precursors for typhoon seasons, fisheries and East Asian summer climate.

### 14. *Nature*: climate change redraws Africa’s childhood malaria map—with opposite east–west effects (Climate · Health)
**Summary:** A Yale-led team in *Nature* used more than a century of clinical data to attribute anthropogenic climate change’s effect on childhood malaria across sub-Saharan Africa. Temperature and extreme precipitation show robust links to prevalence; the historical continental net impact is about 1 excess case per 1,000 children (95% CI −4 to 6), with increases in East and southern Africa offset by declines in hotter West Africa as conditions move past mosquito optima. Over the coming century, climate change could marginally accelerate elimination where today’s burden is highest in West and central Africa; holding warming under 2 °C versus ~3 °C could avert about 5 excess cases per 1,000 by 2100 in high-elevation (>1 km) East Africa and in southern Africa (wide intervals). Yale News, Carbon Brief and others have amplified the findings.

**Links:**

- [Nature — The past and future impact of climate change on childhood malaria in Africa](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10840-w)
- [Yale News — Climate change is likely worsening childhood malaria, study finds](https://news.yale.edu/2026/07/29/climate-change-likely-worsening-childhood-malaria-study-finds)

**Commentary:** “Climate makes malaria uniformly worse” is too blunt—the policy signal is risk migrating into cooler highlands, so elimination and surveillance must move with the map.
