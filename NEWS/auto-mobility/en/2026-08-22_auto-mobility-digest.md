## I. EVs & Policy

### 1. China's largest-ever auto recall: ~4.3M EVs across nine brands, Tesla ~2.98M (Policy / Safety)
**Summary:** On August 21, China's State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) announced that Tesla and eight other automakers—including Xiaomi, Leapmotor, Xpeng, Geely's Zeekr and Lynk & Co, Chery, Dongfeng, BAIC BluePark, and FAW—will recall a combined ~4.3 million electric vehicles in China's largest automotive recall to date. The primary concern is that mechanical emergency door releases may be hard to locate or operate after a severe collision and electrical failure, potentially trapping occupants or blocking rescuers. Tesla's recall covers ~2.98 million imported and China-built Model 3, Model Y, Model S, and Model X, effective September 25; the remedy is free warning labels plus an OTA update that automatically lowers windows after a crash. In a separate action the same day, SAMR ordered an immediate OTA fix for driver-attention monitoring on ~2.74 million China-built Model 3 and Model Y—a distinct defect from the door-handle issue. Regulators also confirmed that concealed door handles will be banned from 2027, making China the first major market to systematically phase out the design.

## II. Supply Chain & Industry

### 3. AI data centers squeeze PCBs and MLCCs, hitting Chinese smart-vehicle makers (Supply chain / China)
**Summary:** The South China Morning Post reported on August 22 that a global shortage of printed circuit boards (PCBs) and multilayer ceramic capacitors (MLCCs), compounded by rising raw-material costs, is disrupting Chinese smart-EV makers' ADAS and cockpit electronics supply. Industry sources cite a ~20%–30% component shortfall globally, with some categories seeing prices more than triple over the past year; normalizing supply could take at least a year. Chen Jinzhu, CEO of consultancy Shanghai Mingliang Auto Service, told SCMP that while end-market EV demand is softening, leading players still lean on intelligence for differentiation—and small-part shortages can halt assembly lines. A Geely Auto executive noted memory chips drive cost pressure, but PCB and MLCC gaps are what disrupt production continuity. The backdrop is AI server and data-center buildouts soaking up high-end MLCC capacity, with Murata's order-to-backlog ratio approaching 2018 shortage-crisis levels.

### 4. Hyundai Motor union's first full-day strike in a decade halts all Korean plants (Industry / South Korea)
**Summary:** On August 21, the Hyundai Motor branch of the Korea Metal Workers' Union staged its first eight-hour full-day strike in ten years, with ~39,000 production and office workers participating and halting plants in Ulsan, Asan, and Jeonju for the entire day. The union is demanding a bonus equal to 30% of last year's net profit and an extension of the retirement age; talks remain deadlocked. Cumulative strike hours this year have reached ~120, with four-hour partial walkouts planned for August 24–25. Seoul Economic Daily estimates the labor action could cost ~62,000 vehicles and ~2.6 trillion won (~$1.9 billion) in lost sales. Roughly 90,000 auto-sector union members joined a joint rally the same day; Kia's union held a six-hour partial strike. The disruption lands as Hyundai pushes new models to revive domestic sales in the second half.

## IV. Charging & Mobility

### 9. ChargePoint deploys overhead 200 kW DC fast charging at Portland airport for rental fleets (Charging / U.S.)
**Summary:** On August 21, ChargePoint completed an overhead DC fast-charging installation at Portland International Airport's Quick Turnaround Facility for rental EVs, with ten dual-port 200 kW dispensers allowing 20 vehicles to stay connected while ten charge simultaneously. Overhead mounting with retractable cable management keeps hardware out of vehicle circulation paths and reduces collision risk; the project reused existing electrical capacity and added spare conduit for future expansion. The system uses ChargePoint Express Plus 1000 hardware with remote monitoring and programmable charge limits by time or state of charge, aimed at shortening turnaround times between rentals.

**Summary:** Per an August 21 NY1 report, Throo—operated by Singapore parent TADA—soft-launched in New York on June 10 and is challenging the Uber/Lyft duopoly by charging drivers a flat $1 per ride instead of the incumbents' typical 25%–30% commission. Former TLC Commissioner Matthew Daus said if drivers embrace the subscription-style fee, incumbents may adjust their models too.
