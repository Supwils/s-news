## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. MIND diet shows greater cognitive benefit for people with higher Alzheimer’s blood markers: post-hoc RCT analysis
**Summary:** Rush University and collaborators reanalyzed the MIND diet randomized trial in about 602 community-dwelling older adults (mean age ~70) without baseline cognitive impairment, comparing three-year global cognition change on the MIND diet versus a control diet. Higher baseline Aβ40 and p-tau181 were linked to more cognitive decline on the control diet but not on MIND, with significant between-group differences in biomarker-related cognitive slopes (Aβ40: β=0.027; p-tau181: β=0.023). The original trial did not show MIND significantly better overall, and this post-hoc analysis cannot prove diet alone blocks decline.

**Links:**

- [Journal of Alzheimer’s Disease — Dietary intervention and cognition across Alzheimer’s disease biomarker levels](https://doi.org/10.1177/13872877261442856)
- [Rush University — Study shows role of MIND diet intervention on cognition](https://www.rush.edu/news/study-shows-role-mind-diet-intervention-cognition)

**Commentary:** If brain health is a priority, treat leafy greens, berries, nuts, and less red meat/sweets as a long-term pattern—not a last-minute fix after a blood-marker report.

### 2. Gut byproduct TMAO linked to higher atrial fibrillation risk: 5,000+ blood samples plus mechanism work
**Summary:** A Cleveland Clinic–led study in the *Journal of Clinical Investigation* analyzed blood from more than 5,000 people undergoing heart procedures and added cell experiments, linking higher trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) levels with elevated atrial fibrillation risk. TMAO forms when gut microbes metabolize nutrients abundant in red meat and other animal products; the work suggests it can alter the heart’s electrical environment via muscarinic receptor–related autonomic dysfunction. The evidence mixes observational association with preclinical mechanism and does not prove any single food causes AF.

**Links:**

- [Cleveland Clinic / Newswise — Gut bacteria byproduct and irregular heart rhythm](https://www.newswise.com/articles/cleveland-clinic-researchers-find-link-between-gut-bacteria-byproduct-and-irregular-heart-rhythm)
- [Medical Xpress — Red meat-related gut byproduct tied to higher atrial fibrillation risk](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-07-red-meat-gut-byproduct-higher.html)

**Commentary:** People with AF or high cardiovascular risk should fold red-meat intake into overall diet and risk management—not overhaul treatment or eliminate all animal foods based on one TMAO reading.

### 3. Night-owl women eat later and show less favorable body fat and metabolic markers: cross-sectional, not causal
**Summary:** A *Frontiers in Nutrition* cross-sectional study of 287 healthy New Zealand women of European and Pacific ancestry (ages 18–45) compared morning, intermediate, and evening chronotypes using five-day food records, body composition, and metabolic biomarkers. Evening types ate more between 8:00 p.m. and 3:00 a.m.; despite similar total daily calories, they had higher body-fat and abdominal-fat measures and less favorable glucose and lipid markers. Authors note chronotype relates to meal timing, but the design cannot prove late eating itself causes obesity.

**Links:**

- [Technology Networks — Night owl eating habits may increase obesity risk](https://www.technologynetworks.com/tn/news/night-owl-eating-habits-may-increase-obesity-risk-414977)
- [Frontiers in Nutrition — Chronotype, meal timing, body composition, and metabolic biomarkers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2026.1862060/full)

**Commentary:** Night owls need not force a morning chronotype; shifting high-energy late snacks earlier and keeping a steadier eating window is a practical first test, checked against waist, weight, and labs.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 4. Self-selected rest between sets is workable but not clearly better than fixed rest: meta-analysis of 16 acute studies
**Summary:** A *PLOS One* systematic review and three-level random-effects meta-analysis pooled 16 acute experiments (281 participants) comparing self-selected versus fixed rest intervals on performance, heart rate, and blood lactate. Overall performance did not differ significantly (g=0.27, 95% CI −0.14 to 0.69), nor did heart rate or lactate; after outlier removal, self-selected rest showed only a small advantage (g=0.20). Heterogeneity was high, and findings apply mainly to acute sessions—not long-term hypertrophy or strength adaptations.

**Links:**

- [PLOS One — Self-selected versus fixed rest intervals: systematic review and meta-analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0354594)

**Commentary:** Recreational lifters can rest until movement quality returns; if self-selected rests blow up session length, add a fixed upper limit for recovery and consistency.

### 5. Eight-week FIFA 11+ improves fatigued landing mechanics in adolescent female soccer players: small RCT
**Summary:** A *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation* randomized trial enrolled 21 adolescent female soccer players (ages 13–17) with dynamic knee valgus and compared 8 weeks of FIFA 11+ with usual warm-ups. The intervention group improved Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) scores after a functional fatigue protocol and attenuated fatigue-related deterioration in landing quality. The sample is small and focused on a biomechanical risk phenotype; better mechanics imply risk-factor improvement, not proven ACL injury-rate reduction.

**Links:**

- [BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil — FIFA 11+ and landing mechanics after fatigue](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-026-01906-3)

**Commentary:** Youth teams and parents should treat structured neuromuscular warm-ups as routine, not a pre-match stretch ritual; keeping landing quality under fatigue matters more than “feeling like I can still run.”

## III. Public Health & Disease Prevention

### 6. WHO updates dementia risk-reduction guidelines: lifestyle and chronic-disease care may address ~45% of modifiable risk
**Summary:** WHO released the second edition of its guidelines on reducing cognitive decline and dementia risk, synthesizing evidence since 2019 and noting that up to about 45% of dementia cases globally may be delayed or prevented. Recommendations cover physical activity, tobacco cessation, reducing harmful alcohol use, healthy diet, cognitive training, and social engagement, plus a new call to reduce air-pollution exposure; hearing support, cardiometabolic management, and multidomain interventions may also be offered. Without a diagnosed deficiency, vitamin B, vitamin E, omega-3, and multivitamin/mineral supplements are not recommended for dementia prevention.

**Links:**

- [WHO — New guidelines: up to 45% of dementia risk could be prevented or delayed](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-07-2026-new-who-guidelines--up-to-45--of-dementia-risk-could-be-prevented-or-delayed)
- [Medical News Today — WHO dementia prevention guidelines update](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/who-dementia-prevention-guidelines-lifestyle-changes-slash-risk)

**Commentary:** The highest-value “brain-health package” for most people remains movement, blood-pressure and glucose control, less smoking/alcohol, social connection, and hearing care—not unindicated “brain” supplements.

### 7. WHO launches PROTECT drowning-prevention package: seven strategies from swim education to flood readiness
**Summary:** Ahead of World Drowning Prevention Day, WHO released the PROTECT technical package translating the global drowning-prevention strategy into seven actionable domains: physical infrastructure for safer water interaction, rescue and resuscitation capacity, occupational safety around water, water-transport standards, basic swimming and water-safety education, child-care and safeguarding systems, and preparedness for floods and multi-hazard threats. The package targets policymakers, but households and communities remain essential implementation partners.

**Links:**

- [WHO — Seven strategies to prevent drowning](https://www.who.int/news/item/23-07-2026-who-launches-seven-strategies-to-prevent-drowning)

**Commentary:** For families, the most direct steps are formal water-safety training for children, continuous adult supervision near water, and learning to call for help and perform CPR—not relying on inflatable toys.

**Summary:** Same-day reporting of CDC data put 2026 U.S. measles cases at 2,318—already above the 2,289 cases recorded for all of 2025 and the highest since 1991—across 35 outbreaks in 43 states plus Washington, D.C., with at least 151 hospitalizations. ### 9. Oral antiviral candidate blocks measles-like virus spread: ferret proof-of-concept, not a human medicine yet
**Summary:** A *Nature Microbiology* study used canine distemper virus in ferrets to model contact and airborne measles-like transmission and tested the broad-spectrum paramyxovirus polymerase inhibitor GHP-88310. Prophylactic dosing around exposure blocked airborne spread; treating infected animals eased illness and shortened the contagious period. Investigators say they are preparing formal human trials. The work shows outbreak-support potential but is not clinical proof and cannot replace vaccination.

**Links:**

- [Nature Microbiology — GHP-88310 blocks transmission in a ferret model of measles-like disease](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41564-026-02419-y)
- [ScienceDaily — Experimental oral antiviral and measles-like virus spread](http://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260724061431.htm)

**Commentary:** Treat this as a promising preclinical tool, not an available therapy; personal prevention still centers on MMR vaccination and prompt public-health or clinical contact after exposure.

### 10. Wildfire smoke linked to more migraine-related emergency visits: nearly 1 million Canadian encounters analyzed
**Summary:** Health Canada researchers examined nearly 1 million emergency visits for migraine and related severe headache in Alberta and Ontario (2010–2023) and found about a 6% increase in headache-related ED trips associated with wildfire-sourced PM2.5. The findings suggest smoke-filled periods may precede severe headache episodes needing emergency care; authors also call for more study of indoor filtration and outdoor protection. CBC reported the results on July 24, 2026.

**Links:**

- [CBC News — Migraine-related hospital visits spike during wildfire smoke](https://www.cbc.ca/news/health/migraine-wildfire-study-9.7282333)

**Commentary:** People prone to migraine should check air-quality indexes on smoke days, limit outdoor exposure, and improve indoor filtration; worsening attacks also warrant medical review of treatment—not just “toughing it out.”

## IV. Sleep & Mental Health

### 11. Insomnia may reflect reduced psychological “integration”: network analysis in 391 students
**Summary:** A *PLOS One* comparative network analysis of 27 transdiagnostic psychological markers in 391 Iranian university students (169 poor sleepers, 222 good sleepers) found poorer sleepers had sparser networks, more isolated nodes, and lower node predictability, without significant global-strength differences between groups. Authors suggest insomnia may involve reduced coordination among cognitive, emotional, and regulatory processes—not only elevated symptom levels. The cross-sectional student sample cannot establish causality or represent all ages and clinical insomnia.

**Links:**

- [PLOS One — Insomnia and psychological disintegration](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0354243)

**Commentary:** Persistent insomnia deserves attention beyond “hours slept”—including stress, mood, and daytime function; if life is disrupted for weeks, prioritize evidence-based options such as CBT-I.

### 12. Microglia drive sleep loss in Alzheimer’s models: temporary depletion restored >2 hours of sleep
**Summary:** University of Kentucky researchers reported in *Alzheimer’s & Dementia* that microglial inflammatory cascades responding to amyloid plaques—not plaques alone or simple neuron death—were primary drivers of sleep loss in Alzheimer-like mice. Temporarily depleting about 87% of microglia with a drug restored more than two hours of sleep per night, lengthened NREM bouts, and did so without changing plaque burden. This is animal mechanism work; the depletion strategy is not a human treatment plan.

**Links:**

- [News-Medical — Microglia found to drive sleep loss in Alzheimer’s](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260724/Microglia-found-to-drive-sleep-loss-in-Alzheimere28099s.aspx)
- [Alzheimer’s & Dementia — Early microglial response to amyloid plaques drives sleep loss](https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/alz.71579)

**Commentary:** For everyday readers, treat sleep problems as actionable brain-health signals worth clinical evaluation—not DIY experiments with experimental anti-inflammatory or immune-cell–depleting approaches.

## V. Supplement Safety & Evidence Clarification

### 13. Salmonella outbreak tied to moringa leaf powder declared over—but long-shelf-life recalls may still be at home
**Summary:** CDC and FDA closed the multistate Salmonella investigation linked to moringa leaf powder products, with a final tally of 131 illnesses in 38 states; of 121 people with information, 36 were hospitalized and none died. Epidemiology, traceback, and lab data implicated multiple greens powders and capsules containing moringa; 12 finished-product or ingredient samples tested positive for Salmonella. Although the outbreak is over, products can remain on shelves for years, and CDC still urges discarding or returning recalled lots.

**Links:**

- [CDC — Salmonella outbreak linked to moringa leaf powder](https://www.cdc.gov/salmonella/outbreaks/supergreenssupplementpowders-1-26/index.html)
- [Food Safety News — Outbreak traced to moringa leaf powder declared over](https://www.foodsafetynews.com/2026/07/outbreak-traced-to-moringa-leaf-powder-declared-over/)

**Commentary:** “Natural superfood” is not a sterility guarantee; keep lot numbers for powders and capsules and check recalls yourself—don’t assume a closed outbreak means leftover jars are safe.
