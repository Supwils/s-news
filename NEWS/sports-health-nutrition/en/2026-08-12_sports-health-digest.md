## I. Nutrition & Diet

### 1. Meta-analysis: ~28 g nuts per day linked to ~20% lower hypertension risk (Nutrition)

**Summary:** Published online Aug 4, 2026 in the *British Journal of Nutrition*, a systematic review and dose–response meta-analysis searched through April 2026 and included eight prospective cohorts with 143,380 participants and 20,665 hypertension cases. Highest versus lowest nut intake showed a summary RR of 0.84 (95% CI 0.77–0.91); each ~28 g/day increment had RR 0.80 (95% CI 0.71–0.89). Non-linear curves flattened around 25–30 g/day; WCRF criteria graded causality as probable. News-Medical summarized the work on Aug 12, 2026. This is observational synthesis, not proof nuts replace antihypertensive drugs.

**Links:**

- [British Journal of Nutrition — Nut intake and hypertension meta-analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/nut-consumption-and-the-risk-of-hypertension-a-systematic-review-and-dose-response-metaanalysis-of-prospective-studies/F598AFD9FD35B00A41A428C9D65F2C14)
- [News-Medical — A handful of nuts and blood pressure (2026-08-12)](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260812/How-much-do-nuts-matter-for-blood-pressure-This-study-points-to-about-a-handful-a-day.aspx)

**Commentary:** Swap unsalted nuts in for ultra-processed snacks rather than eating extra nuts on top of a salty diet—people with hypertension still need meds and sodium control per clinician guidance.

### 2. 12-week RCT: three daily full-fat dairy servings did not meaningfully raise weight or lipids in adults with overweight (Nutrition)

**Summary:** Harvey Anderson's team at the University of Toronto reported in *The Journal of Nutrition* (2026) a randomized trial of 74 adults with overweight or obesity assigned to low-dairy calorie restriction, energy-neutral three dairy servings/day, or unrestricted intake with three dairy servings/day for 12 weeks. No meaningful differences emerged in weight, body composition, or blood cholesterol; higher dairy groups showed blood-pressure improvements and higher calcium, protein, and vitamin D intake. ScienceDaily covered the work Aug 9; AgriMoon on Aug 12. Small sample and short duration— not a verdict that full-fat dairy is universally superior.

**Links:**

- [The Journal of Nutrition — Full-fat dairy 12-week RCT (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tjnut.2026.101373)
- [ScienceDaily — Full-fat dairy, weight, and lipids](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/08/260808234948.htm)

**Commentary:** If you tolerate dairy, fit portions into overall calories instead of fearing saturated fat alone; lactose intolerance or dyslipidemia still warrants individualized choices.

### 3. NHANES: higher ultra-processed food calories tied to ~24%–31% higher prostate cancer risk in U.S. men (Nutrition / Public health)

**Summary:** Charles Hennekens and colleagues published in *The American Journal of Medicine* (DOI: 10.1016/j.amjmed.2026.07.043), analyzing 17,024 U.S. men ≥18 years in NHANES 2003–2023 using two 24-hour recalls and NOVA classification into four UPF calorie-share groups (~<20% to >44%). Men in the three higher UPF groups had ~29% greater prostate cancer risk; the two highest groups ~30%; adjusted increments ranged ~24%–31%. Authors call for larger observational studies and trials; prior meta-analyses on prostate cancer endpoints have been inconsistent. Newswise reported in August 2026.

**Links:**

- [Newswise — Ultra-processed foods and prostate cancer risk](https://www.newswise.com/articles/study-links-ultra-processed-foods-to-significantly-higher-prostate-cancer-risks)
- [The American Journal of Medicine — Original article (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2026.07.043)

**Commentary:** For men, cutting UPF supports broader metabolic and cancer-prevention goals—keep age- and risk-appropriate PSA/digital screening on schedule rather than treating diet change as a substitute.

### 4. Pilot RCT: 8–9-hour eating window beat 12-hour window on spatial planning in postmenopausal women with overweight (Nutrition / Brain health)

**Summary:** Sue Shapses' Rutgers team presented at NUTRITION 2026 an unpublished peer-review 6-month pilot: 47 women aged 50–79 with overweight/obesity all cut ~500 kcal/day; 26 limited eating to ~8.2 hours (≈10:00–18:00) and 21 kept ~12.3 hours. Both lost ~15 lb, but the shorter window group scored significantly better on spatial planning and problem-solving; memory-learning errors trended lower (not significant); reaction time and multitasking did not differ. SAMAA TV recirculated Aug 12, 2026. Tiny sample—not a dementia-prevention prescription.

**Links:**

- [ScienceDaily — 8–9-hour eating window and cognition (NUTRITION 2026)](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/07/260727012125.htm)
- [SAMAA TV — Time-restricted eating and memory in older women (2026-08-12)](https://www.samaa.tv/2087355063-time-restricted-eating-linked-to-better-memory-in-older-women)

**Commentary:** If you already cut calories, try an earlier last meal and a 4-hour pre-bed fast and track sleep and daytime focus—don't treat conference pilot data as Alzheimer's protection.

## II. Exercise & Training Science

### 5. Meta-analysis: SportsMetrics training improves ACL-related biomechanical surrogates, not yet injury incidence (Training / Sports medicine)

**Summary:** Published Aug 8, 2026 in *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation*, a systematic review and meta-analysis through May 2026 included nine studies and 310 athletes (mostly female). SportsMetrics training (SMT) correlated with better dynamic knee valgus (SMD −1.09), Landing Error Scoring System scores (SMD −1.08), and agility, balance, speed, and power; core strength changes were non-significant. GRADE certainty was low to very low; designs were largely quasi-experimental with no prospective injury registration. Authors warn short-term movement gains must not be read as ACL risk reduction.

**Links:**

- [BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil — SportsMetrics training meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-026-01948-7)

**Commentary:** Use SMT-style drills within warm-ups, but program purchases should still prioritize neuromuscular packages with documented injury-rate drops (e.g., FIFA 11+), not LESS scores alone.

### 6. 24-week RCT: complex training boosted jump height and eccentric strength; contrast training cut push-off time (Training)

**Summary:** The Aug 2026 *Journal of Strength & Conditioning Research* reported a 24-week randomized trial in 45 national-level male track athletes split into complex training (postactivation potentiation), contrast training (postactivation performance enhancement), or control. Complex training outperformed on arm-swing jump height (~+30.9%) and drop height (~+30.4%); contrast training excelled at reducing push-off time without arm swing (~−6.1%) and raising push-off power (~+17.1%). Eccentric strength explained ~43% of jump-height variance. Elite lab data—not a general gym template.

**Links:**

- [JSCR — Complex vs contrast training 24-week RCT (DOI)](https://doi.org/10.1519/jsc.0000000000005451)

**Commentary:** Train elastic rebound with complex heavy-to-explosive pairings; chase contact-time and rate with contrast sets—they target different qualities, so program accordingly.

### 7. 12-week lab trial: VR-assisted neuromuscular training improved agility and knee-valgus markers in basketball players (Training)

**Summary:** Published Aug 5, 2026 in *Scientific Reports*, 70 university basketball players were randomized to VR-assisted neuromuscular training, conventional training, or control for 12 weeks. The VR group showed larger agility gains (p<0.001; d ~0.89–1.34), ~18.7% vs 8.2% dynamic knee-valgus reduction (p<0.001), and ~27.2% vs 12.3% reactive-strength-index gains. Single-center lab work without season injury counts.

**Links:**

- [Scientific Reports — VR neuromuscular training in basketball](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-48999-x)

**Commentary:** VR can supplement standard warm-ups and strength work, but most teams should master landing control and load management before buying headsets.

### 8. Mice: acute and chronic exercise modulate skeletal-muscle supersulfide metabolism and antioxidant capacity (Training / Mechanism)

**Summary:** Published Aug 6, 2026 in *Scientific Reports*, 10-week-old male mice underwent single-bout or 4-week training protocols. Acute exercise raised CARS2 and GSSSH; training raised CARS2, sulfide ions, cysteine, and GSSG, increased persulfidation and activity of mitochondrial ATP synthase α, and improved superoxide and hydrogen-peroxide scavenging. Animal mechanism work—doses do not map directly to human exercise prescriptions.

**Links:**

- [Scientific Reports — Exercise-induced supersulfide metabolism in muscle](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-65857-y)

**Commentary:** Another molecular "why exercise helps" pathway—consistent aerobic plus resistance beats hunting supersulfide supplements.

### 10. NHIS cross-section: cardiovascular disease linked to lower mammogram uptake (Public health / Screening)

**Summary:** A cross-sectional analysis in *Journal of Medical Screening* pooled 2019, 2021, and 2023 U.S. National Health Interview Survey data: ~73.1% had up-to-date colorectal screening and 77.8% mammogram uptake. Cancer history raised colorectal screening odds (OR 1.74); cardiovascular disease without cancer history lowered mammogram odds (OR 0.79, 95% CI 0.68–0.91). Authors call for targeted breast-screening outreach among CVD patients. Cross-sectional association only.

**Links:**

- [SAGE Journals — Cancer history, CVD, and colorectal/breast screening](https://sage.cnpereading.com/doi/10.1177/09691413251388517)

**Commentary:** If you see cardiology regularly, don't treat that as a reason to skip annual mammograms—bundle cancer screening into chronic-disease follow-up lists.

## IV. Sleep & Mental Health

### 11. Meta-analysis of 11 RCTs: non-drug sleep–circadian interventions reduce depression severity across mental disorders (Sleep / Mental health)

**Summary:** Published Aug 6, 2026 in *Frontiers in Psychiatry*, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 RCTs in adults with depression, bipolar disorder, severe mental illness, ADHD, and mixed samples found combined sleep–circadian interventions linked to lower depression severity (SMD −0.51, 95% CI −0.72 to −0.30), higher response (RR 1.68), and better insomnia, sleep quality, and functional outcomes. GRADE certainty ranged low to moderate; some endpoints rested on only two trials. Search through April 12, 2026.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers in Psychiatry — Sleep–circadian interventions and depression meta-analysis](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1901978/full)

**Commentary:** In stable depression or bipolar care, fixed wake times, morning light, and pre-bed screen cuts belong alongside meds and therapy—not as extra sedatives by default.

### 12. Six-month cohort: baseline insomnia severity predicts suicide attempts in depressed youth (Sleep / Mental health)

**Summary:** Published Aug 2, 2026 in *Child and Adolescent Psychiatry and Mental Health*, a Chinese psychiatric-hospital cohort followed 561 depressed adolescents for six months; 81.6% met insomnia criteria at baseline (AIS ≥6). Multivariable Cox models showed each one-point increase in insomnia severity associated with HR 1.07 for suicide attempts (95% CI 1.01–1.13); depression severity and lifetime suicidality also predicted attempts. Single-center observational work with modest effect size.

**Links:**

- [CAPMH — Insomnia severity and suicide attempts in depressed youth](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13034-026-01148-8)

**Commentary:** Adolescent depression visits should routinely ask sleep hours and bedtimes; worsening insomnia warrants contacting clinicians—not self-dosing melatonin.

## V. Supplements & Food Safety

### 13. EU RASFF analysis: diverse online supplement hazards; two-thirds classifiable with a food-regulation decision tree (Evidence / Food safety)

**Summary:** Published July 27, 2026 in the *Journal of Consumer Policy*, a secondary analysis of 910 EU RASFF e-commerce notifications (Nov 2017–Jun 2023) found 786 food-related alerts and 689 supplement-related ones; common hazards included 2,4-dinitrophenol, CBD, and yohimbine. Authors built an international–EU–member-state decision tree classifying about two-thirds of supplement hazards under food rules and flagged gaps for botanicals and online channels. Regulatory epidemiology—not a consumer testing guide.

**Links:**

- [Journal of Consumer Policy — EU online supplement RASFF decision tree](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10603-026-09628-2)

**Commentary:** When buying fat-loss, sleep, or muscle capsules online, choose labeled ingredients from reputable channels—skip DNP, mystery botanical stacks, and miracle claims.

### 14. Swiss market survey: only 18.4% of fermented plant "yogurts" fortified; 1:1 swap cuts calcium, iodine, B12 (Nutrition / Evidence)

**Summary:** A *Frontiers in Nutrition* cross-sectional survey of 98 fermented plant-based yogurt products in Swiss retailers (2025) found soy (36.6%) and coconut (26.8%) bases dominant. Coconut options were more energy-dense with higher saturated fat and lower protein; only 18.4% were micronutrient-fortified. Substitution modeling showed 1:1 replacement of dairy yogurt without fortification lowered calcium, iodine, phosphorus, zinc, and vitamins A, B2, B5, and B12, with soy raising folate slightly. Single-country snapshot—not universal to all brands.

**Links:**

- [Frontiers in Nutrition — Swiss plant-based yogurt nutrition and fortification](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2026.1899857/full)

**Commentary:** Read labels for calcium and B12 fortification—plant-based marketing alone doesn't guarantee adequacy; vegan and low-dairy eaters should track iodine and B12 totals.
